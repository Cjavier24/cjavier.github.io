Honestly, I was already familiar with email. I've written a lot of emails throughout my life, and they are really useful whenever I need to send a formal message or attach files. However, there are a couple of things that I didn't know before reading the article. First, I sent an Email to myself (it was one of the activities). I did know how to send an email; it's easy. What I didn't know was that I could unsend them! When I read that in the tutorial, I was stunned. It's definitely a feature that I wish I had known years ago. Second, I tried to set up a Thunderbird account. I thought this service would be similar to Gmail or Outlook, but it looks like it serves a different purpose. The process of making an account appears to be a little different from the video tutorial in the article because it's so old, but I managed to create one anyway. I have to say, the interface is clean, and I like it. This application might be especially valuable for me since I manage many different emails at the same time, including my Harper College email. Next, I didn't know what an Internet mailing list was, so I decided to subscribe to one. I specifically wanted to find one related to Chess, but most of them have been replaced by forums like Chess.com or Lichess. However, these websites have account settings that allow them to send chess puzzles directly to your email if you enable them. It looks pretty fun and, as a chess fan, I couldn't resist trying it out! On the other hand, even though I don't think the average user would use an Internet mailing list, I did some research and found that some communities still use them, so it's helpful to know about them. Finally, I learned new terms such as BCC, Mail Delivery Agent (MUA), netiquette, and more. All this terminology makes me wonder if there's a word or abbreviation for everything on the Internet.
